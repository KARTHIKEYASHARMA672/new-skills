# Editing Examples

Examples of appropriate edits at each pass level, showing when to use track changes vs comments.

---

## Pass 1: Structural (Comments Only)

**Example 1 — Redundancy**
Original text discusses same concept in Chapter 2 and Chapter 5.
Comment: "This section covers similar ground to §2.3. Consider consolidating or cross-referencing to avoid repetition."

**Example 2 — Gap**
Argument jumps from premise to conclusion without connecting logic.
Comment: "The leap from 'AI can process data quickly' to 'AI should make hiring decisions' needs intermediate steps. What's the reasoning chain?"

**Example 3 — Pacing**
Three chapters of background before the main argument begins.
Comment: "Consider whether all this background is needed upfront. Could some be moved to an appendix or introduced later when relevant?"

---

## Pass 2: Line Editing (Track Changes + Comments)

**Example 1 — Tightening**
Original: "It is important to note that the system has the ability to process"
Edit (track change): "The system can process"

**Example 2 — Clarity**
Original: "This is something that educators should think about carefully."
Edit (track change): "Educators should consider this carefully."

**Example 3 — Awkward construction**
Original: "The reason why this matters is because students learn better when engaged."
Edit (track change): "This matters because students learn better when engaged."

**Example 4 — Ambiguity requiring author input**
Original: "The results were promising for the technology."
Comment: "Promising in what sense? Speed? Accuracy? Cost? Consider being more specific."

---

## Pass 3: Copy Editing (Primarily Track Changes)

**Example 1 — SPAG**
Original: "Their going to need more training then expected."
Edit (track change): "They're going to need more training than expected."

**Example 2 — Consistency**
Style guide says "machine learning" not "ML".
Original: "ML models require significant data."
Edit (track change): "Machine learning models require significant data."

**Example 3 — Fact query**
Original: "The study, published in 2019, found..."
Comment: "Please verify — I'm seeing references suggesting this was 2018."

**Example 4 — Missing word**
Original: "The teacher asked students complete the assignment."
Edit (track change): "The teacher asked students to complete the assignment."

---

## Comment Tone Examples

**Good comments — concise, actionable:**
- "Unclear antecedent — does 'it' refer to the model or the data?"
- "This contradicts §3.2. Which is correct?"
- "Consider cutting — this point was made in the previous paragraph."
- "Verify date."

**Bad comments — lecturing, vague:**
- "Pronouns should always have clear antecedents to avoid confusing readers. Here, the word 'it' could potentially refer to either the model or the data, creating ambiguity."
- "This section could perhaps be improved in some way."
- "I'm not sure about this."

---

## When NOT to Edit

**Preserve author voice:**
Original: "And here's the thing. AI isn't magic."
Don't change sentence fragments if they're deliberate style.

**Don't over-polish:**
Original: "The results were good."
If context makes "good" clear and the tone is intentionally casual, don't change to "The results demonstrated statistically significant improvements."

**Subjective preferences:**
Original: "However, the data suggests..."
If you prefer "The data suggests, however,..." — don't change. Comment only if there's a genuine clarity issue.
